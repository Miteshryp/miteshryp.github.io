## A Lathe for the Mind
```verse
The prepared mind, sooner or later finds something important and does it. So yes, it is luck. The particular thing you do is luck, but that you do something - is not.
                                                                                             ~Richard Hamming

```

The above is a quote from the Turing Award Winner Richard Hamming's famous lecture named "_You and Your Research_"<sup>[[3](#bibliography)]</sup>, which was delivered by him in 1986 during his stint as a Professor at the University of Virginia. If you are someone who is not well acquianted with prominent computer science legends, this is the same Hamming who invented "Hamming codes", which today forms the basis of error-correction and plays an extremely critical function in telecommunications and satellite systems.

Now I will be honest from the get-go: I have neither watched the full lecture given by Hamming, nor have I read the transcript of this lecture; I was rather introduced to this quote from an inspiring talk<sup>[[2](#bibliography)]</sup> by [Prof. Uday Khedkar](https://www.cse.iitb.ac.in/~uday/) given during the IICT 2026 conference. Something about this quote has since stuck with me, maybe because it reflects one of my central principles about work ethics, and the general nature of science. 

Hence through this blog post, my goal is to draw your attention onto a conversation about the world of research and science, how it is fundamentally different from engineering, my journey and experience being surrounded by it for the past year, the purview of education that I have developed after being exposed to the academic world, and lastly my (extremely un-important) opinion on the progression of human knowledge in a world which seems to be ever-more driven by both useful AI tools as well as the slop that comes with it.

The quote reasons about the role of luck in our lives as we work towards our goals. I believe it wouldn't wrong to assume that most points in the curve of our lives are a function of luck and timing, and no matter how hard we try, things will always be a subject to this human flaw of the world. However, this outlook towards life can very easily put us in harms way - by making us believe that efforts are worthless - that no matter what we do or how much effort we put into things, they do not affect the outcome. Keep this in your mind for a while as you read on, we'll circle back to this [@TODO: circle back - discuss the idea of efforts being worthless in the age of AI]

Something similar has been happening to our thought processes and attention spans since the December of 2025, when a cocktail of RL and LLMs gave rise to agentic AI, and suddenly everyone in the industry seems to have gone mad in terms of their expectations and fancies. In this environment of constant chaos, pressure and (unrealistic) expectations - it becomes even more easier to think that if most our work is just clicking OK on a claude session - what work are we really doing? And at some level, this is an extension of the thought that if we are driven by luck, what use is investing in effort of learning and understanding anything?

Prof. Uday Khedkar has tried to tackle an aspect of this argument from POV of researchers, as he describes the core essence of Research in his talk - in a rather beautiful way. One of these beautiful idea's which had caught my eye was that of how research is more about rigour than about creativity.
